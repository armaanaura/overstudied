Confirmation bias is the tendency to seek, interpret, and remember information in ways that support an existing belief. It does not mean people deliberately ignore reality; it often operates through ordinary choices about where to look and what to question.

## How the bias appears

Once we form a hypothesis, supporting examples become easier to notice. Contradictory evidence may receive stricter scrutiny, while agreeable evidence feels plausible without the same examination.

Online search can amplify the effect. Two questions about the same claim can produce very different evidence:

- “Why does this method work?”
- “What evidence would show that this method fails?”

## Confidence is not evidence

A coherent explanation can feel true because its pieces fit together. That feeling is useful for generating hypotheses, but it does not establish that the explanation matches reality.

Good reasoning separates three things: the claim, the evidence, and the confidence justified by that evidence.

## Practices that reduce the effect

No checklist removes bias completely, but a few habits make it easier to detect.

- Write down what evidence would change your mind
- Search deliberately for the strongest competing explanation
- Ask someone to challenge the assumptions, not just the conclusion
- Prefer predictions made before observing the result
- Track failed predictions as carefully as successful ones

## Productive disagreement

The goal of disagreement is not to defend an identity. Restating another position in a form its supporters recognize helps reveal whether the conflict concerns facts, definitions, priorities, or values.

Intellectual honesty does not require having no convictions. It requires allowing evidence to influence how strongly those convictions are held.
