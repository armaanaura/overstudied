Reading something repeatedly can make it feel familiar, but familiarity is not the same as being able to recall it later. Spaced repetition improves retention by turning review into a series of effortful retrievals spread across time.

## The forgetting curve

New information becomes harder to retrieve when it is not used. The rate of forgetting is not identical for every person or every idea, but the general pattern is useful: memory fades quickly at first and more slowly afterward.

A review interrupts that decline. When the review successfully retrieves the idea, the next period of useful retention usually becomes longer.

## Retrieval is the important part

Effective review is not simply seeing the answer again. Trying to produce an answer before revealing it strengthens access to the memory and exposes what is still unclear.

Useful prompts include:

- Explaining an idea without looking at your notes
- Solving a problem from a blank page
- Predicting the next step in a process
- Comparing two concepts that are easy to confuse

## Why spacing helps

An immediate review is often too easy because the answer is still active in working memory. Waiting introduces desirable difficulty: recall requires more effort, but the memory is not yet completely inaccessible.

As recall becomes reliable, the interval can grow. A simple schedule might revisit an idea after one day, three days, one week, and several weeks. The exact numbers matter less than adapting the interval to recall quality.

## A practical study loop

Turn each important idea into a focused question. Attempt the answer, check it, and record whether the response was correct and complete. Schedule difficult material sooner and familiar material later.

Spaced repetition works best alongside understanding. Create connections, work through examples, and use retrieval practice to keep those connections available over time.
