Working memory can hold and manipulate only a limited amount of information at once. Cognitive load theory helps explain why a technically correct explanation can still feel impossible to follow.

## Three sources of cognitive load

Intrinsic load comes from the inherent complexity of the material and the learner's existing knowledge. Learning recursion, for example, requires coordinating several ideas that eventually become a single familiar pattern.

Extraneous load comes from presentation choices that do not help learning. Unexplained notation, constant context switching, and diagrams separated from their labels all consume attention.

Germane processing is the effort used to organize ideas into durable mental models. Comparing examples or explaining why a solution works can support this process.

## Prior knowledge changes difficulty

Experts group many details into meaningful chunks. A beginner sees individual syntax rules, variables, and control-flow steps; an experienced developer may see one familiar algorithm.

This is why an explanation should establish prerequisites before introducing several interacting abstractions. What looks concise to an expert can be densely packed for a newcomer.

## Designing clearer explanations

A useful lesson reduces avoidable load without removing the thinking that creates understanding.

- Introduce one important idea at a time
- Place labels next to the part of a diagram they describe
- Use a worked example before asking for an independent solution
- Remove information that does not support the immediate goal
- Summarize how the parts connect before adding more detail

## Applying it to self-study

When a topic feels overwhelming, split it into smaller dependencies. Externalize intermediate values on paper, keep a glossary for new terms, and practice one variation before combining several.

The goal is not to make every task effortless. It is to reserve mental effort for the structure of the idea rather than for avoidable confusion.
